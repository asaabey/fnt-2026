Q2 — Renal sodium conservation after gastrointestinal bleeding
Correct answer: D

Slide 1: Renin release after blood loss
Correct answer: D. ↓ NaCl transport at the macula densa → ↑ renin release from juxtaglomerular granular cells.

Why it is correct: Blood loss, hypotension and tachycardia indicate ↓ circulating volume. ↓ renal perfusion + ↑ upstream sodium reabsorption → ↓ NaCl delivery and transport at the macula densa. Result: ↑ renin release from nearby granular cells. Angiotensin II and aldosterone then support sodium retention; the low urine sodium is consistent with renal conservation.

Learning point: Use the clinical evidence of volume loss to infer the renal response. Macula-densa cells sense NaCl; juxtaglomerular granular cells release renin.

Slide 2: Macula-densa NaCl transport and renal perfusion pressure
A — Macula-densa signal direction
Why it is wrong here: ↑ macula-densa NaCl transport → ↓ renin release. Volume depletion → ↓ NaCl signal → ↑ renin release.
Learning point: ↓ macula-densa NaCl → ↑ renin; ↑ NaCl → ↓ renin.

B — Afferent arteriolar stretch
Why it is wrong here: Haemorrhage → ↓ renal perfusion + ↓ afferent arteriolar stretch. ↓ perfusion pressure → ↑ renin; ↑ stretch → ↓ renin.
Learning point: The renal vascular signal responds to perfusion pressure, not to plasma sodium concentration alone.

Slide 3: Sympathetic beta-1 regulation of renin release
C — Sympathetic beta-1 signalling
Why it is wrong here: Blood loss → ↑ renal sympathetic activity. ↑ beta-1 stimulation → ↑ renin release; ↓ beta-1 stimulation removes a renin stimulus.
Learning point: Sympathetic beta-1 input reinforces the low-perfusion and low-NaCl signals.

Take-home: ↓ renal perfusion + ↓ macula-densa NaCl transport + ↑ beta-1 stimulation → ↑ renin release. This is an appropriate response to volume loss.

Q3 — Hypertension with hypokalaemia
Correct answer: A

Slide 1: Aldosterone-mediated sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion
Correct answer: A. ↑ apical ENaC activity + ↑ basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase activity.

Why it is correct: The findings suggest primary aldosteronism. Aldosterone → ↑ ENaC-mediated sodium entry + ↑ basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase activity. Sodium retention contributes to hypertension. Electrogenic sodium reabsorption makes the lumen more negative, favouring potassium secretion through apical potassium channels and explaining hypokalaemia.

Learning point: Connect the two clinical findings to one pathway: ↑ distal sodium reabsorption supports volume expansion and potassium loss.

Slide 2: AQP2 water permeability and Na+/K+-ATPase transport
B — Aquaporin-2 and water handling
Why it is wrong here: Aquaporin-2 is primarily regulated by vasopressin. Water-channel insertion alone does not explain aldosterone-driven electrogenic sodium uptake and renal potassium loss.
Learning point: Distinguish vasopressin/AQP2 water handling from aldosterone/ENaC sodium and potassium handling.

C — Basolateral pump inhibition
Why it is wrong here: Na+/K+-ATPase inhibition → ↓ gradient for sodium entry + ↓ potassium uptake into principal cells. It opposes the transport changes expected with aldosterone excess.
Learning point: The basolateral pump sustains both sodium reabsorption and the cellular potassium supply for secretion.

Slide 3: ENaC activity and potassium secretion
D — ↓ ENaC activity
Why it is wrong here: ↓ ENaC activity → ↓ electrogenic sodium uptake → less negative lumen → ↓ driving force for potassium secretion. This is the opposite of the mechanism causing her findings.
Learning point: ENaC inhibition can promote natriuresis and spare potassium; ↑ ENaC activity favours potassium loss.

Take-home: High aldosterone with suppressed renin suggests renin-independent aldosterone production. Hypokalaemia is a useful clue but is absent in many patients with primary aldosteronism.

Q7 — Oedema with hyponatraemia
Correct answer: B

Slide 1: Hypervolaemic hypotonic hyponatraemia
Correct answer: B. ↑ total-body sodium + ↑ water; proportionately greater ↑ in water.

Why it is correct: Weight gain and worsening oedema in this patient favour sodium and water retention. Low measured osmolality confirms hypotonic hyponatraemia. Proportionately greater ↑ in water than in exchangeable sodium + potassium → ↓ plasma sodium despite ↑ sodium stores.

Plasma [Na⁺] ≈ (Naₑ + Kₑ) / TBW
Naₑ and Kₑ are exchangeable sodium and potassium; TBW is total-body water.

Learning point: Hyponatraemia does not necessarily mean sodium depletion. In the Gamblegram: ↑ cation bar, with a proportionately greater ↑ in water.

Slide 2: Hypovolaemic hyponatraemia and sodium-predominant retention
A — Sodium and water depletion
Why it is wrong here: This pattern can cause hypovolaemic hyponatraemia, but it does not explain her weight gain and progressive oedema as well as retention. ↓ effective arterial filling in nephrotic syndrome is not the same as depleted total-body sodium.
Learning point: Assess sodium stores separately from effective circulating volume.

C — Sodium gain exceeding water gain
Why it is wrong here: With exchangeable potassium held constant in the simplified model, this would cause ↑ cation-to-water ratio, favouring hypernatraemia, whereas her plasma sodium is low.
Learning point: The direction of the sodium change depends on the relative changes in cations and water.

Slide 3: Sodium and water balance in oedematous disease
D — Retention with preserved concentration
Why it is wrong here: This can produce weight gain and oedema with normal plasma sodium, as in the first stage of the Gamblegram’s oedema panel. It cannot account for her hypotonic hyponatraemia.
Learning point: Sodium retention can accompany normal, low or high plasma sodium, depending on water balance.

Take-home: Read concentration and volume separately. The animation contrasts water-predominant retention in SIADH, unreplaced water loss in DI, and sodium plus water retention in oedematous disease. Here, water gain outpaces cation gain.

Q12 — IgA nephropathy and infection-associated haematuria
Correct answer: A

Slide 1: IgA nephropathy: presentation and mesangial IgA
Correct answer: A. Dominant or co-dominant mesangial IgA deposition

Why it is correct: Recurrent glomerular haematuria during or shortly after respiratory symptoms suggests IgA nephropathy. Its defining biopsy finding is dominant or co-dominant mesangial IgA. Normal serum C3 supports the pattern but does not establish the diagnosis; clinical suspicion and biopsy confirmation are distinct.

Learning point: Use timing to form a hypothesis and the biopsy pattern to establish the lesion.

Slide 2: Linear IgG and full-house immunofluorescence
B — Linear IgG
Why it is wrong here: Strong linear IgG along the GBM is characteristic of anti-GBM antibody disease, rather than the expected mesangial immune-deposit pattern in this case.
Learning point: Linear binding along a fixed basement-membrane antigen differs from mesangial immune-complex deposition.

C — Full-house staining
Why it is wrong here: Full-house staining includes IgG, IgA, IgM, C3 and C1q and commonly supports lupus nephritis in the right context. It is not the expected dominant mesangial IgA pattern.
Learning point: Full-house staining is a contextual clue, not a standalone diagnosis of systemic lupus.

Slide 3: Pauci-immune glomerulonephritis
D — Pauci-immune injury
Why it is wrong here: Scant immune deposits with necrotising crescentic GN are typical of an ANCA-associated pattern. IgA nephropathy is instead defined by mesangial IgA deposits.
Learning point: Pauci-immune means few immune deposits; severe inflammation and necrosis can still be present.

Take-home: Compare deposit pattern and location: mesangial IgA, linear GBM IgG, full-house immune deposits, or a pauci-immune lesion.

Q13 — Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis
Correct answer: D

Slide 1: Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis
Correct answer: D. Immune-mediated glomerular injury with complement activation and typically granular immune deposits

Why it is correct: A nephritic presentation around two weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis, with low C3, supports post-streptococcal GN. Glomerular immune-mediated injury activates complement and typically produces granular immune deposits. The preceding infection triggers the immune process; it does not imply suppurative infection of the kidney.

Learning point: A latent interval after infection supports a post-infectious mechanism, in contrast to near-concurrent haematuria in typical IgA presentations.

Slide 2: Immune-mediated GN and direct renal infection
A — Direct bacterial invasion
Why it is wrong here: The case describes a delayed nephritic syndrome with red-cell casts and low C3. Direct suppurative renal infection is a different process; it does not explain the characteristic immune-mediated glomerular pattern.
Learning point: Distinguish an infection triggering GN from bacteria directly infecting urinary or renal tissue.

B — Anti-GBM antibodies
Why it is wrong here: Antibody binding to fixed type IV collagen antigens produces a linear anti-GBM pattern. The post-infectious pattern here instead favours immune-mediated injury with granular deposits.
Learning point: Linear and granular staining reflect different patterns of immune involvement.

Slide 3: Complement and immunofluorescence in GN
C — ANCA-associated injury
Why it is wrong here: ANCA-associated GN is usually pauci-immune. The latent post-streptococcal course with low C3 is more consistent with the immune-deposit/complement pattern in answer D.
Learning point: Integrate the history, complement and biopsy pattern; low C3 alone is not specific for one GN.

Take-home: Post-streptococcal GN is one infection-related GN. Other infections and other immune diseases can also cause low complement and glomerular injury.

Q14 — Lupus nephritis and the role of kidney biopsy
Correct answer: C

Slide 1: Lupus nephritis: biopsy class, activity and chronicity
Correct answer: C. Arrange prompt nephrology assessment and kidney biopsy, if feasible, to assess class, activity and chronicity.

Why it is correct: Red-cell casts, substantial proteinuria and declining kidney function raise concern for active lupus nephritis. If feasible, kidney biopsy helps identify the histological class and assess active inflammation versus chronic scarring. These findings inform treatment decisions beyond what the established systemic lupus diagnosis alone provides.

Learning point: Assess the kidney lesion, not just whether the patient has systemic autoimmunity.

Slide 2: Serology and proteinuria in lupus nephritis
A — Anti-dsDNA titre alone
Why it is wrong here: Anti-dsDNA and complement can support assessment of lupus activity, but they cannot reliably assign the renal histological class or quantify chronic scarring.
Learning point: Serology complements renal assessment; it does not replace histological classification when biopsy is indicated.

B — Waiting for nephrotic-range proteinuria
Why it is wrong here: Active lupus nephritis can occur with sub-nephrotic proteinuria. Red-cell casts and a creatinine rise already make this presentation significant.
Learning point: The nephrotic-range threshold defines a protein-excretion category, not the threshold for investigating inflammatory kidney disease.

Slide 3: Ultrasound and kidney biopsy in lupus nephritis
D — Ultrasound versus microscopy
Why it is wrong here: Ultrasound assesses macroscopic anatomy, including kidney size and obstruction. It cannot resolve glomerular immune deposits or assign lupus-nephritis class.
Learning point: Light microscopy assesses structural lesions; immunofluorescence assesses immune staining; electron microscopy adds ultrastructural detail.

Take-home: Link urinary sediment and kidney-function change to prompt assessment. Biopsy class, activity and chronicity help guide care when biopsy is feasible.

Teacher references: R8

Q15 — ANCA-associated crescentic glomerulonephritis
Correct answer: B

Slide 1: PR3-ANCA-associated necrotising crescentic GN
Correct answer: B. Necrotising crescentic glomerulonephritis with scant or absent immune deposits

Why it is correct: Upper-airway symptoms, haemoptysis and rapidly declining kidney function with PR3-ANCA strongly suggest ANCA-associated vasculitis. The expected renal lesion is necrotising crescentic GN with scant or absent immune deposits. Possible pulmonary haemorrhage and rapid renal decline require urgent specialist assessment.

Learning point: A pauci-immune biopsy can show severe necrosis and crescent formation.

Slide 2: Crescentic GN: light microscopy and immune deposits
A — Linear GBM IgG
Why it is wrong here: Linear IgG would raise anti-GBM disease, including a possible overlapping antibody syndrome. It is not the expected pauci-immune pattern of the PR3-associated presentation.
Learning point: ANCA positivity alone does not exclude anti-GBM overlap; reconcile serology with biopsy.

C — Mesangial IgA
Why it is wrong here: Mesangial IgA supports IgA nephropathy. It is less consistent with this combined upper-airway, pulmonary and rapidly progressive PR3-associated presentation. IgA disease can itself sometimes develop crescents.
Learning point: Do not classify the cause of GN from crescents alone; deposit pattern and clinical context matter.

Slide 3: Immune-deposit patterns in GN
D — Full-house immune deposits
Why it is wrong here: A full-house pattern commonly supports lupus-associated immune-complex disease. Substantial granular immunoglobulin/complement deposits are not the expected pauci-immune pattern here.
Learning point: Immune-complex GN and pauci-immune GN can both be severe; the distinction concerns immune deposits, not simply severity.

Take-home: Crescents describe severe glomerular injury. Combine light microscopy, immunofluorescence, serology and the clinical course to identify its cause.

Q16 — Anti-GBM disease and antibody-directed injury
Correct answer: A

Slide 1: Anti-GBM antibodies and linear IgG staining
Correct answer: A. Autoantibodies directed against a type IV collagen antigen in the basement membrane

Why it is correct: Strong linear IgG in a crescentic pulmonary-renal presentation supports anti-GBM disease. The classic antibody target is the non-collagenous domain of the alpha-3 chain of type IV collagen. Binding along the basement membrane explains the linear pattern. The disease requires urgent assessment.

Learning point: Connect the immune target to the staining pattern: antibody binding along a fixed GBM antigen produces linear staining.

Slide 2: Mesangial IgA deposition and podocyte dysfunction
B — IgA immune complexes
Why it is wrong here: IgA-containing immune complexes produce the characteristic mesangial deposit pattern of IgA nephropathy. They do not explain strong linear IgG along the GBM.
Learning point: The identity, location and distribution of immune deposits all contribute to the diagnosis.

C — Isolated slit-barrier dysfunction
Why it is wrong here: Podocyte slit-barrier dysfunction helps explain protein leakage, but by itself does not explain linear GBM IgG, crescentic injury and pulmonary haemorrhage.
Learning point: A structural barrier abnormality is not a substitute for identifying the immune mechanism causing the injury.

Slide 3: Anti-GBM disease and ANCA-associated pulmonary–renal syndromes
D — ANCA-mediated injury
Why it is wrong here: ANCA-associated vasculitis can also cause rapidly progressive GN and pulmonary haemorrhage. Its usual renal pattern is pauci-immune, whereas this biopsy has strong linear GBM IgG.
Learning point: Clinical syndromes may overlap; immunofluorescence and antibody testing help distinguish anti-GBM from ANCA-associated disease.

Take-home: Both anti-GBM disease and ANCA-associated GN may cause pulmonary-renal syndromes. The tissue immune pattern is a major discriminator; overlap can occur.

Teacher references: R10, R6, R9
