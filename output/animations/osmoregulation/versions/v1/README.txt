OSMOREGULATION: VASOPRESSIN, URINE CONCENTRATION AND THIRST

Captioned, silent Manim animation, matching the dark background and Avenir
Next typography of the existing renal animations.

Sequence
The scene opens at low plasma osmolality. A shared cursor sweeps right,
revealing three response curves. Water falls while the displayed sodium
particle count remains fixed. Vasopressin rises, urine becomes concentrated,
and thirst increases in the classic teaching schematic. The animation pauses
at the renal concentrating ceiling: vasopressin continues to rise while urine
osmolality plateaus. A drinking-water illustration appears. After absorption,
the cursor returns left along the fully visible curves, water rises, and the
drive to retain and drink water falls. The final state illustrates movement
toward balance rather than return to the initial low-osmolality condition.

Interpretation
The shared horizontal axis is plasma osmolality, not time. Animation duration
does not represent biological response time. The three vertical axes have
independent scales. Thirst is relative intensity; it is not measured in the
vasopressin or urine units. The blue curve represents vasopressin concentration,
not a range of normal values. The orange plateau represents a maximum renal
concentrating response, not saturation of vasopressin secretion.

The curves are drawn schematically, informed by the user-supplied figure;
their equations are illustration functions, not fitted data. In particular,
AVP onset around 280 and thirst onset around 290 mOsm/kg are illustrative
features of that classic schematic. Thresholds vary between individuals and
experimental methods. Thirst and AVP thresholds can overlap; renal saturation
need not precede thirst. The animation deliberately shows thirst developing
before the orange curve is completely flat. The figure should not be used
to diagnose a patient, infer exact hormone levels, or predict urine output.

The vessel illustrates water relative to sodium. Particle number stays fixed;
water height and particle density are qualitative and not a quantitative
compartment or mass-balance model. Counterions and other osmoles are implicit.
The renal meter follows the illustrative urine-concentration response and is
not a measured water reabsorption rate. Urine-volume descriptions presume
approximately constant osmole excretion and intact renal concentrating ability.
Early anticipatory effects of drinking on thirst and AVP, volume-mediated AVP
release, glucose and urea effects, and disease states are outside this scene.
