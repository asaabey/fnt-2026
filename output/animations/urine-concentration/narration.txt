A concentrating kidney needs two things: an osmotic gradient in the medulla,
and a collecting duct that allows water to leave.

In the thick ascending limb, NKCC2 supports net salt reabsorption. This segment
is water-impermeable, so water does not follow the salt across its wall.
The loop system contributes to the gradient toward the deeper medulla.

High vasopressin keeps collecting-duct water pathways available. With a strong
gradient, water can leave the collecting duct and be recovered.

A loop diuretic inhibits NKCC2. Less salt is reabsorbed into the medulla,
weakening the gradient that drives water recovery.

Notice what stays the same: vasopressin is still high, and the water pathways
remain open. But a permeable pathway cannot replace the osmotic driving force.

The result is reduced maximum urine-concentrating capacity. The gradient is
weakened, not completely eliminated. That is why high vasopressin cannot fully
preserve concentration after NKCC2 blockade.
