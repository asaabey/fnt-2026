Opening
Glomerular pressure depends partly on resistance before and after the
capillary filter. The afferent arteriole is the inlet; the efferent is the
outlet. Each comparison starts from its own reference setting.

Angiotensin II
At moderate activation in low perfusion, preferential efferent constriction
helps support filtration pressure. Strong activation has broader effects.

ACE inhibitors
Reducing angiotensin II formation relaxes the efferent arteriole. Initial
filtration can fall even while vascular blood flow continues. Lower
intraglomerular pressure can be protective over time in suitable patients.

ARBs
Blocking the AT1 receptor reduces angiotensin-mediated efferent tone through
a different molecular target, with a similar illustrated vascular endpoint.

Prostaglandins and NSAIDs
In a low-perfusion setting, prostaglandins help keep the afferent inlet open.
NSAIDs inhibit their production. Losing this vasodilator support can tighten
the inlet and reduce filtration.

SGLT2 inhibitors
More sodium chloride reaches the macula densa, restoring feedback in the
classic hyperfiltration model. Afferent tone increases and excessive
intraglomerular pressure falls. Some type 2 diabetes studies support an
efferent-dilation contribution instead. The mechanism is context-dependent.
An early haemodynamic dip can accompany long-term kidney protection.

Calcineurin inhibitors
Acute toxicity from tacrolimus or ciclosporin can cause afferent-predominant
vasoconstriction. The actual vascular response can be more complex.

Combination
Volume depletion reduces perfusion. Add loss of afferent dilation from an
NSAID and loss of efferent constrictor support from an ACE inhibitor OR ARB:
filtration may fall substantially. Diuretic-associated volume depletion
acts through perfusion, not a simple selective arteriolar switch.

Summary
The direction of the pressure change is only the start of the interpretation.
Clinical context, magnitude and time course determine what it means.
